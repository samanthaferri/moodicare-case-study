The Context

The WHO estimated that in 2021 more than one billion people lived with a mental disorder, roughly 14% of the world's population. Continuous care is hard to sustain, and two everyday problems keep undermining it. They are interdependent, yet most tools treat them apart.

The Problem

Behind those numbers, two everyday problems keep undermining continuous treatment. They are interdependent, yet most tools handle them apart.

Problem 01

Medication slips through the cracks

People forget doses, lose track of complex schedules, and drift out of treatment. Low adherence is one of the main obstacles to clinical results in long treatments, and it raises the risk of relapse.

Problem 02

Emotional history goes unrecorded

Mood variation stays poorly systematized outside the clinic. People arrive at appointments without records and unsure what to say, which makes individualized decisions harder for both sides.

Research question

How can a mobile app, grounded in usability and user-centred design, integrate emotional monitoring and medication-adherence support in a way that is accessible, consistent, and useful for clinical follow-up?

The Solution

Two things that shape a treatment, medication and mood, usually live in separate apps. Moodicare puts them on the same timeline: reminders, dose tracking, a daily mood log, integrated analytics, and an exportable clinical report, in one accessible experience. It is a complement to professional care, never a diagnostic tool.

Methodology

The project follows user-centred design (ISO 9241-210) and sets concrete accessibility and privacy targets as requirements, because it handles sensitive health data. Six principles guided every screen.

Research → Define → Ideate → Design system → Hi-fi flows → Validate

S

Remove needless complexity so the daily routine stays light and easy to keep up.

♥

Soft shapes and calm colour lower the resistance people feel toward a health app.

Consistency

A documented design system keeps behaviour predictable across the app.

Requirements

Research and definition converged into a set of requirements: what the product must do, and the qualities it was required to hold because it handles health data.

Functional What it does

  • Account & access: sign up and log in with Google or email, recover password.
  • Medication registration: five posology modes covering real prescriptions.
  • Reminders: doses, missed-dose alerts, stock and mood check-ins.
  • Dose logging: on-time, late, extra, ignored, and reschedule.
  • Mood logging: 1–10 scale, feelings, and free-text notes.
  • Integrated calendar: medication and mood on one timeline.
  • Analytics & report: adherence and mood, exportable as a clinical PDF.
  • Management: suspend, resume or delete medications, keeping history.

Non-functional How it should behave

  • Accessibility: WCAG 2.1 contrast and legibility set as targets.
  • Privacy: LGPD-aligned handling of sensitive data.
  • Usability: grounded in ISO 9241-210.
  • Consistency: a reusable component library with defined states.
  • Plain language: conversational UX writing over clinical jargon.

Validating the Proposal

Before building the experience, the concept was tested. A structured questionnaire with 65 respondents — Information Systems students and health professionals — gauged routines, familiarity with health apps, and interest in the proposed features. This was exploratory: it measured declared interest and perceived usefulness, not usability, task completion, or clinical impact. The app was not published to the stores or used by anyone outside the team.

01

Onboarding & account creation

Goal: explain the value first, ask for personal data second.

Three welcome screens introduce the two axes and their integration before a single field is requested, so the first impression is value, not a demand. Only then does the app move into account creation: sign up with Google or email, verify the email through a segmented code input, and complete a short profile laid out for calm, sequential reading.

  • Value-first onboarding lowers the sense of immediate demand and builds trust.
  • Segmented code fields reduce typos and reinforce a sense of security.
  • A preferred form of address feeds personalization from the first screen.
  • Automatic redirect from onboarding to sign-up keeps the experience unbroken.

Onboarding: the three structuring ideas, shown before any sign-up.

Account creation: sign up, verify by code, complete a minimal profile, land on a confirmation.

02

Login & password recovery

Goal: fast, repeatable re-entry, with self-service recovery that never dead-ends.

Login is deliberately direct, because it repeats over time and shapes how reliable the app feels. Recovery is a calm four-step sequence: enter the email, confirm a code (with resend if it expires), set a new password with real-time feedback on each requirement, then a clear success screen back to login.

  • "Remember me" keeps sessions active on trusted devices without extra friction.
  • A resend option on the code screen prevents users from getting stuck.
  • Live password validation shows each requirement being met as you type.
  • Every step ends in an explicit confirmation, so state is never ambiguous.

Login, empty and filled, with a path into recovery.

Password recovery: email, code confirmation, new password with live feedback, and success.

03

Home & daily navigation

Goal: understand today's treatment at a glance, and act in one tap.

The home screen greets users by their chosen name, opens with a one-tap mood check-in, and presents the day's medication agenda in chronological order. Every dose card carries a clear visual state, and a floating button opens the most frequent actions.

  • Tapping a mood face jumps straight into logging with the scale pre-positioned.
  • Dose cards encode five states (pending, on-time, ignored, off-schedule, unregistered).
  • Distinct empty states guide first-time users and quiet days without feeling broken.
  • A floating action button centralizes "add medication", "extra dose", and "log mood".

Home: from empty state, to a full agenda, plus the quick-action sheet.

04

Mood logging

Goal: capture emotion with warmth and enough depth to be useful, without feeling like a form.

Logging happens in three progressive steps: a 1-to-10 intensity slider mapped to five soft categories (with adjustable date and time for retroactive entries), an optional multi-select of feeling chips, and an optional free-text note. A number, categories and words together keep the record analyzable but free to express.

  • Quantitative, categorical, and textual data in one record widen interpretation.
  • Feelings can be added, renamed, reordered, hidden or removed; renames propagate to past records.
  • Deleting a feeling explains the impact and lets the user keep or erase associated history.
  • Multiple entries per day capture real oscillation instead of flattening it.

Mood logging in three steps: intensity, optional feelings, optional note.

Managing feelings, with clear handling of what happens to history on rename or delete.

05

Medication registration

Goal: model a real prescription, so reminders, adherence and reports all follow from it.

This was the hardest design problem in the project. Real prescriptions vary enormously: a daily pill, an antibiotic every eight hours, an injection every few days, medication only on specific weekdays, cycles of use and pause, or something taken only when needed. Each pattern implies different reminders, adherence math, and calendar behaviour.

Deep dive · the posology system

One consistent shell, only the recurrence changes.

I kept a single sequential structure for every medication — identification, then mode of use, then times and doses, then treatment duration, then optional stock control. Only the recurrence step differs between patterns. That preserved a familiar rhythm across all cases while still honouring the real logic of each prescription, which lowers configuration complexity and prevents inconsistent data downstream.

Daily useEvery day, one or more times, freely adjustable.
Regular intervalsEvery X days or hours, auto-calculated from an anchor.
Specific weekdaysChosen days only; changes affect future schedules.
CyclesX days of use, Y of pause, auto-restart, no pause alerts.
When neededNo reminders; logged only via manual extra doses.

For hourly intervals, calculated times are locked while per-dose quantity stays editable, protecting the prescribed logic from accidental drift. Optional stock control estimates consumption and warns before you run out.

  • Starts with the pharmaceutical form, since it defines the route and everything downstream.
  • Only truly essential fields are required; concentration and notes stay optional.
  • Mode of use is asked in plain language: "How did your health professional ask you to take this?"
  • Schedule edits apply to future occurrences only, preserving the historical record.

The shared shell, shown here for daily use: form and name, mode of use, times and doses, duration and stock.

Same rhythm, four different recurrences: every X hours, every X days, specific weekdays, and cycles.

06

Extra doses

Goal: record what happens outside the plan without corrupting the plan.

Sometimes a dose is taken off-schedule, or a professional advises an occasional extra. The user picks an active medication, confirms quantity, date and time (pre-filled with the current moment but fully editable), and the dose enters history classified by its temporal status. Crucially, it never rewrites the configured schedule.

  • Only active medications appear, with a clear empty state and a shortcut to add one.
  • The selected medication stays pinned at the top to prevent mis-entry.
  • Pre-filled but editable date and time support immediate and retroactive logging.
  • Keeping extra doses separate protects the integrity of reports and indicators.

Logging an extra dose: choose the medication, enter the details, confirm into history.

07

Integrated calendar

Goal: one timeline for both medication and mood, so the picture stops being fragmented.

This is where the two axes finally meet. A day-of-week strip and a monthly view sit above a "Medication / Mood" tab switch that keeps the selected date when you move between them. On the medication side, tapping a dose opens a bottom sheet to Ignore, Take (now, on-time, or a custom time), or Reschedule. On the mood side, every entry is editable and deletable, with multiple records per day.

  • Switching tabs preserves the date, keeping medication and mood in the same context.
  • "Take" distinguishes now, on-time, and a custom time, so delays are honest data.
  • "Ignore" records a deliberate omission, different from simply having no record.
  • Rescheduling is validated and generates a fresh reminder, so nothing falls through.

Medication calendar: dose states and the take / ignore / reschedule bottom sheet.

Mood calendar: chronological entries on the same date, each editable and deletable. (Source screen still shows placeholder chip and note text — flagged for cleanup.)

08

Indicators & clinical report

Goal: turn daily records into patterns a person and their doctor can actually read.

The analytics layer offers weekly and monthly views: adherence as a donut and per-medication rate, weekday bars, average mood with its category, distribution by mood category, and the most frequent feelings. As-needed medications show only what actually happened, so they never distort adherence. From here, the user exports a clinical report as a PDF for a chosen period, structured for professional follow-up. It informs interpretation, it does not replace it.

  • Two perspectives on adherence, overall and per-medication, avoid a single misleading number.
  • Doses confirmed more than 30 minutes late still count as taken, but are flagged distinctly.
  • A monthly mood grid relates weeks and weekdays, surfacing patterns at a glance.
  • The exportable report gives clinicians objective, period-based data to work from.

Weekly indicators: adherence, weekday completion, mood average and feelings.

Monthly indicators: a full-month summary, per-medication grids, and mood across weeks.

The exported clinical report (sample data): overview and adherence, then mood analysis.

09

Medication management

Goal: consult, edit, pause and resume prescriptions without ever losing history.

Medications are split into "in use" and "suspended". A detail screen separates general information from the prescription itself. Users can edit from a pre-filled form, suspend a medication so it drops out of schedules, resume it later restoring its schedule, or delete it, always with an explicit choice to keep or erase the associated history.

  • Suspend and resume model real treatment interruptions without deleting anything.
  • Delete forces a conscious choice between keeping records or erasing them for good.
  • The detail screen mirrors how a prescription is actually reasoned about.
  • A persistent add shortcut lives even in the empty state.

Managing medications: in-use and suspended lists, detail view, and suspend / resume.

10

Settings, notifications & privacy

Goal: give people real control over identity, reminders, and what shows on a lock screen.

Settings group account and profile management alongside institutional information and support. The most considered part is notifications. Reminders cover doses, missed doses (with a configurable tolerance window), a "take your meds when leaving home" nudge, stock replenishment, and mood check-ins. Because these alerts can appear where other people might see them, the user can hide medication names in notifications, swapping them for neutral messages — privacy by design tied to sensitive health data.

  • A privacy toggle hides medication names on the lock screen, protecting data in shared spaces.
  • A configurable tolerance window defines when a dose counts as "possibly forgotten".
  • Reminders are granular, so people enable only what actually helps them.
  • Terms of use and privacy policy are surfaced in-app, not buried.

Reflection & Next Steps

What worked

Anchoring every screen to user-centred principles kept the interface calm under real complexity. Medication registration is the clearest example: a genuinely intricate problem made routine by holding one structure steady and letting only the recurrence change.

What I would do differently

Validate earlier and with real users. Personas grounded in desk research are a reasonable start, but talking to people in continuous treatment would sharpen priorities and surface edge cases I can only hypothesise today.

What is next

Usability testing of the prototype, then development in React Native and Laravel as planned in the thesis. Beyond that, the respondents' own ideas: personalised recommendations, clinical integration, and light gamification.

What I learned

Designing for health means designing for trust. Small decisions — hiding a medication name on a lock screen, letting someone keep their history when they delete a drug — are where an app earns the right to hold sensitive data.
